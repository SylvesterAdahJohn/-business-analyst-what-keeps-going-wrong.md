# What keeps going wrong

*A short review note, written the way I would send it to a project lead. The numbers and examples are invented for this repository. They are not from any real project.*

---

I went through twenty AI answers against our six-rule guideline. Fourteen of them had at least one problem. Here is what I think is behind them, and what I would fix first.

## What I found

Seventeen problems across those fourteen answers:

- **Wrong facts:** 6
- **Missing information:** 5
- **Instructions not followed:** 4
- **Tone or format:** 2

The wrong facts are the loud ones, so they get the attention. The missing information worries me more. Those answers read well and sound complete, and a reviewer in a hurry would pass them. If we only count what is wrong and never what is absent, we will keep approving answers that quietly leave things out.

## Where the guideline let us down

Two rules caused most of the disagreement, and neither problem was really about the reviewers.

**Completeness.** The rule says to "cover the main points", but it never says how many is enough. One answer covered three of four points. One reviewer passed it and another failed it, and both had a fair reading of the rule. That is a rule problem, not a reviewer problem.

**Sources.** The rule asks for a "reliable source" but does not say whether a company's own help page counts as primary. One reviewer accepted a third-party blog because nothing said not to.

## What I would change first

1. Give the completeness rule a clear pass line, such as "every point named in the prompt".
2. List the source types we accept, with one example of each.
3. Add three borderline answers to the guideline, with the decision and the reason for each, so reviewers have something to measure against.

After that, I would have the same reviewers score a fresh batch of answers and see whether they now agree with each other. If they do, the rules were the problem. If they don't, we need to look somewhere else.

## What I would want to know next

How many reviewers worked on this batch, and how often did they land on the same decision? Twenty answers is enough to spot a pattern, but not enough to be confident about how big it is.
